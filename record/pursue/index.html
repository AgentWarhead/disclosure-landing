The PURSUE UFO files

Six releases since May 2026, published by the Department of War at war.gov/UFO. What PURSUE is, what each batch held, and what none of it shows.

Record / PURSUE file releases / Released in partReleased in partCivilian copy

The short answer

PURSUE is the Department of War’s program for releasing government UAP files. Since May 8, 2026 it has published six batches at war.gov/UFO. The files are real government records. None of them is an official finding that anything was non-human.

PURSUE stands for Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters. The releases follow the President’s February 19, 2026 statement that he would direct agencies to find and release their UAP files. Every event here also sits in the Disclosure Ledger, with its verdict.

What PURSUE is

Full name
Presidential Unsealing and Reporting System for UAP Encounters
Run by
The Department of War, with support from the Office of the Director of National Intelligence. The first release named the White House, ODNI, the Energy Department, AARO, NASA and the FBI among the partners.
First release
May 8, 2026
Where
war.gov/UFO, searchable by release, agency and file type, with downloads for each release
Pace
Rolling, with new batches posted every few weeks, according to the department

The department says the archive holds unresolved cases: reports where the government could not determine what was seen, often for lack of data. Cases AARO resolves are reported separately, in its annual reports.

A PURSUE Task Force also exists. A law firm summary dates its start to May 1, 2026. We could not confirm that date in any government or major news source, so we do not use it.

What the files do and do not show

The files are worth reading. They are also easy to oversell. This is what the department and the record actually support.

What they show

  • Real government records: documents, photos, videos and audio from the Pentagon, NASA, the FBI, the CIA, the Energy Department and other agencies.
  • Decades of reports. The sixth release alone holds records dated from 1952 to 2025, according to CBS News.
  • Cases the government says it could not resolve, often because there was not enough data.

What they do not show

  • Any official finding that an object was non-human or extraterrestrial. No release statement makes that claim.
  • Finished analysis. The department said many of the materials had not yet been analyzed for resolution.
  • Proof of origin for every file. The Pentagon said many second-release materials lacked a substantiated chain of custody, and that sixth-release reference papers should not be read as current validation, according to CBS News.

Released in part

PURSUE is not the National Archives collection

The two get mixed up constantly, and a lot of wrong headlines start there. The May 8, 2026 release was the Department of War. The National Archives runs a separate UAP Records Collection that Congress created in 2023.

How to read a released file

  1. Check both dates. The date of the incident and the date of the document are different things. A 2026 release can hold a 1952 record.
  2. Find who wrote it. A pilot’s report, an analyst’s note, an FBI interview summary and a reference paper are different kinds of evidence.
  3. Separate the report from the finding. A file that records what a witness saw is a record of the report, not proof of the thing.
  4. Look for what is missing. Redactions, missing sensor data, no chain of custody.
  5. Rule out the ordinary first. Balloons, satellites, aircraft and drones. AARO’s FY2025 report resolved 114 new reports that way.
  6. Check the claim before you share it. The Disclosure Ledger marks what the record confirms and what it does not.
