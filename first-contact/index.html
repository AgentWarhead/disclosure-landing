What to do in the first minutes of first contact.

If contact ever happens near people, the first one there will not be a general or a scientist. It will be a civilian with a phone. This is the briefing for that person.

File DSC-020 / First contact briefing / Released in partReleased in partCivilian copy

Slow down, keep your distance, get the vulnerable away, stay quiet and non-threatening, record only what you can prove, and give each person one job. Do not approach it, chase it, shout at it, shine lights at it, or touch anything it leaves behind.

No government has published a civilian protocol for contact, and every official report so far, from NASA’s 2023 study to the Pentagon’s UAP office, has found no evidence of extraterrestrial technology. So treat this as a drill. It is built from the same habits that keep people safe in any strange, frightening emergency, and it costs nothing to know.

The protocol, on one page.

Six steps, in order. Read them once now, while nothing is happening. That is the only time they are easy.

Civilian contact protocol DSC-020Page 1 of 1

First minutes. Civilian use.

  1. Stop the rush.

    Breathe out slowly. Stop sudden movement. Keep the group from walking toward it. A crowd copies the calmest person or the loudest one, so be the calm one.

  2. Make distance.

    Do not approach. Put space between people and whatever it is, then widen it. You do not know what it is, so you do not know what distance is safe.

  3. Move the vulnerable first.

    Children, pets, anyone frozen or panicking. Get them behind solid cover or indoors, away from windows, before anyone goes back to look.

  4. Go quiet and visible.

    No shouting, no chasing, no flashlights pointed at it, nothing thrown. Keep your hands open where they can be seen. Nothing sudden.

  5. Record what you can prove.

    Time, place, direction, light, sound, movement, weather, who was there, and what your devices did. Write what you saw apart from what you think it was. Do not fill the gaps.

  6. Give everyone one job.

    One person watches the group. One keeps the room calm. One records. One keeps the way out clear. Nobody tries to do all four.

What counts as first contact.

First contact means the first confirmed interaction between people and a non-human intelligence. It would not have to look like a film. It could be a signal picked up by a telescope, a probe, a craft, a public announcement, or something that happens in one field on one night in front of a handful of witnesses.

This briefing covers the last kind, because it is the only one where an ordinary person’s first minute decides anything. A signal from light years away gives everyone decades to argue. A light over the treeline gives you seconds.

Why civilians need their own protocol.

The plans that exist were written for institutions. Astronomers have agreed steps for confirming and announcing a signal (the SETI post-detection protocols). Governments have committees, hearings and records laws. None of them tell the person standing closest to something strange what to do with their hands.

Official help also takes time to arrive. In that gap, the outcome depends on whoever happens to be there, and on whether anyone in the group has thought about it before.

Why each step works.

Slow first, because panic travels.

People in a frightening situation look at each other to decide how scared to be. One person walking calmly, speaking quietly and breathing slowly gives the rest of the group something to copy. One person sprinting gives them something else.

Distance, because you cannot take a step back.

You can always move closer later. You cannot undo having walked up to something you did not understand. Anything unknown, from a downed aircraft to a fallen satellite to something nobody has a word for yet, is safer seen from further away.

Quiet, because everything is a signal.

A shout, a flashlight in the face or a thrown object reads as aggression to a frightened neighbor, a police officer or anything else. Open hands and a still body are the closest thing people have to a universal sign for no threat.

Evidence, because memory is not.

Memory fades within hours and gets rewritten every time the story is retold. A written time, a direction and a steady recording made on the night are worth more than any confident account a week later. The evidence checklist covers what to capture and how.

One job each, because everyone assumes someone else has it.

In a crowd, responsibility spreads so thin that nobody acts. Naming the jobs (watch the group, calm the room, record, keep the exit) turns a crowd back into people who know what to do next.

What the public record says.

The question has moved from late-night radio into sworn testimony, federal law and government archives. The key dates:

  • July 26, 2023. Three former military officers testified under oath before a House Oversight subcommittee about unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP).
  • September 14, 2023. NASA’s independent study team published its UAP report, asked for better data, and found no evidence that UAP are extraterrestrial.
  • December 22, 2023. The defense bill became law and ordered a UAP Records Collection at the National Archives. It was a reduced version of the proposed UAP Disclosure Act.
  • March 8, 2024. The Pentagon’s UAP office (AARO) published a historical review going back to 1945 and found no evidence of extraterrestrial technology.
  • February 19, 2026. The President said he was directing agencies to release UAP files.
  • May 8, 2026. The Department of War published its first batch of declassified UAP files.
  • July 22, 2026. The House passed a UAP Disclosure Act amendment. It still needs the Senate and is not law.

None of these documents confirm contact. They confirm the question is now official, and that nobody official has written the civilian part yet.

How to prepare before it matters.

Under stress, people do not rise to the plan they imagined. They fall back on whatever they have practiced. So practice, briefly and on purpose:

  • Read the six steps above until you could say them without looking.
  • Run the ten-second drill: six decisions from one blackout night, with a clock on each.
  • Talk it through once with the people you live with, the same way you would plan a fire exit. The intel file on a family sighting protocol is a good script.
  • Find the job you reach for first, so you know which one you have to learn to hand off.
