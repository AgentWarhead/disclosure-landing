The AARO report for 2025, explained

AARO’s fiscal 2025 report counted 319 new UAP reports and resolved 114, all as ordinary objects. Here is what it says, and what is still open.

File 069 · Public record · 7 min read · Filed 29 Sep 2026

File INT-069 / The AARO report for fiscal 2025 / UnclassifiedReleased in partCivilian copy

The AARO report for 2025 is the Fiscal Year 2025 Consolidated Annual Report on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena. It covers UAP reports from June 2, 2024 to May 30, 2025. AARO, the Pentagon’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office, received 319 reports in that window and resolved 114 of them, every one as an ordinary object. It found no evidence that anyone has ever captured or exploited UAP material.

The report came out in July 2026 under the Department of War name. The Black Vault dated its release to July 21, 2026 and noted it arrived months after the deadline set in law (The Black Vault). It is short, 13 numbered pages including a glossary, and written for Congress. Here is what it says, in its own numbers.

Quick answer

319 new reports. 114 resolved as balloons, satellites, birds, aircraft, drones, one rocket launch and one jet pack. 256 older cases also resolved. 191 moved to an archive for lack of data, and 9 sent to specialists for more analysis. The report found no evidence of captured UAP material or of health effects, and no resolved case showed breakthrough technology.

What the 2025 AARO report covers

Congress requires the report under section 3373(k) of Title 50 of the U.S. Code. The law asks for every UAP event reported in the year, older events not covered before, analysis of the data, incidents over restricted airspace, possible threats, any activity tied to foreign governments, efforts to capture or exploit UAP, health effects, and incidents near nuclear sites (AARO FY2025 report, section II).

The report’s information cut-off is May 30, 2025, so it says nothing about the 2026 file releases. AARO drafted it with the military services, the Department of War and intelligence agencies. For what the office is and how it works, read what is AARO?

How AARO resolved the cases

The report says all resolved cases are attributable to prosaic objects such as balloons, satellites, birds, aircraft and drones, with one case put down to a commercial rocket launch and one to a manned jet pack. The breakdown by object type appears in a chart, and we do not repeat figures from it here.

Satellites did much of the work. All 44 space-domain reports came from civilian pilots through the FAA (42) or ground-based U.S. Space Command sensors (2), none from space-based sensors, and AARO assessed each with high confidence as satellite flaring. Flaring is sunlight reflecting off a satellite’s surface at the right angle. The report says a flare can look like an intense orb many times brighter than the stars, lasting from a fraction of a second to several minutes. A new three-dimensional modeling tool let analysts resolve 238 reported UAP as satellite flaring. The report gives that figure and the 44 separately and does not say how they relate. The Starlink or UFO file covers how to spot a satellite yourself.

Altitude is another clue. Most airborne reports sat between 10,000 and 35,000 feet. Reports above 45,000 feet fell from 27 percent in the fiscal 2024 report to 4 percent, which AARO attributes to a batch of high-altitude FAA reports in the earlier year. Many of those pilot reports turned out to be satellite flares.

What remains unresolved

  • 191 archived reports. The active archive holds cases with too little data to say whether the event fits natural phenomena or goes beyond known technology. They stay open if better data or methods appear.
  • 9 reports for specialists. AARO sent them to intelligence and science partners with the right technical expertise.
  • A Navy case off Virginia. The year’s single maritime report came from Navy assets and described about 100 airborne UAP and two surface systems that were likely uncrewed. AARO says it is actively investigating with the reporting unit.
  • Two interference reports. Two reports described electronic or avionic interference near an operating aircraft. AARO has not yet decided whether UAP caused it.
  • Reports near sensitive sites. Some narrative reports near national security sites described performance beyond the known state of the art. No technical data came with them, and AARO says that if validated they could be an unmitigated threat.

The report names the underlying problem: a lack of timely and actionable sensor data still limits what AARO can resolve. Its holdings also lean toward places with dense U.S. military sensors, which report more.

What the report says about aliens, crashes and whistleblowers

The word extraterrestrial does not appear in it. What it says instead:

  • Recovered material. No evidence suggests that a government or private entity has ever captured or exploited UAP-derived materials. AARO is drafting a process for handling such material should it ever come into government hands.
  • Technology. None of the resolved cases indicates advanced foreign capabilities or breakthrough technologies in any domain.
  • Health. No reporter has described adverse health effects from a UAP encounter.
  • Whistleblowers. 262 people contacted AARO through its secure reporting form. AARO judged 255 out of scope and asked 7 for follow-up interviews.
  • Drones near nuclear sites. Nuclear agencies passed on 50 reports of drones near U.S. nuclear infrastructure, up from 18 the year before. None was reported as UAP, and quadcopters were the most common type.

How the report relates to the UFO files

The annual report and the 2026 UFO files are two halves of one system. The department says the files at war.gov/UFO are unresolved cases, and that resolved cases are reported separately, as the law requires (war.gov/UFO). This report is that separate reporting. Read together, the release pages list what nobody could explain, and this report shows how often an explanation turns up once the data is good enough. For the six releases, see the PURSUE UFO files, and for the claims made about them, did the UFO files confirm aliens?

Common questions

What is the AARO report for 2025?

The Fiscal Year 2025 Consolidated Annual Report on Unidentified Anomalous Phenomena, which AARO sends to Congress each year. It covers reports from June 2, 2024 to May 30, 2025 and was published in July 2026.

How many UAP cases did AARO resolve in 2025?

AARO resolved 114 of the 319 new reports it received, and 256 earlier cases, for 370 in the period. It says all of them were ordinary objects such as balloons, satellites, birds, aircraft and drones.

Did the 2025 AARO report find aliens?

No. It says none of the resolved cases showed breakthrough technology, and that no evidence suggests anyone has ever captured or exploited UAP-derived materials. 191 reports remain archived for lack of data.

When did the 2025 AARO report come out?

In July 2026. The Black Vault dated the release to July 21, 2026, months after the deadline set in law. The report’s own information cut-off is May 30, 2025.
